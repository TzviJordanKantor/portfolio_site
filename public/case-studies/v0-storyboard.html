The bet

From a plugin to a SaaS company

Elementor was transforming from a WordPress plugin into a hosting & SaaS business. The company objective — “Transform from a plugin to a SaaS company” — meant growing cloud-subscription share and, above all, revenue per user. Staging was one of the adaptive, higher-tier features meant to deepen how integrated a customer was in the ecosystem, and to justify premium plans.

The content problem

An engineer's feature, a designer's audience

Staging is a dev-native concept — staging environments, deployments, push and pull. Elementor's audience is largely non-technical WordPress creators. If the language reads like DevOps, adoption dies at the first modal. The job: make an engineer's feature legible to a designer's audience — without dumbing it down.

The work

A language hierarchy, consistent across every state

I researched industry-standard terminology, then built a naming system that bridges the WordPress/Elementor world with dev-native speak — applied to every modal, empty state, and error, so the feature reads as one coherent voice.

The impact

People actually used it

In the first month of testing, staging hit 12.5% adoption — 197 of 1,551 eligible sites live, with 78 push-to-live and 194 pull-to-staging actions. In targeted cohorts it grew roughly five-fold across two quarters. Over the same period ARPU climbed to $147 (April) from a $127 year-to-date base, as the shift toward value-per-user took hold. Staging shipped to production and became a standard feature on the paid tiers.

Honest framing: content design supported the ARPU/value strategy — I don't claim it caused the revenue lift. The metric I own is adoption of the feature my content made usable.

What it shows

Content as infrastructure

The gap between a feature that ships and a feature people use is usually language. Getting the words right — as a system, not a string — is what turned a technical hosting feature into an adopted one, inside a company betting its future on becoming a SaaS.
